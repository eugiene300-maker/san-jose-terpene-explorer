# UGA Study: Legal Medical Cannabis and Sick Days—Limits Included

*University of Georgia researchers linked medical cannabis law timing to fewer health-related absences. Recreational laws did not show the same effect—and the paper is not a doctor’s note.*

KTVU on Aug. 29, 2026, highlighted a University of Georgia study linking legal marijuana access to fewer sick days—a workplace-wellness angle that travels easily through Bay Area feeds. Open-web coverage from FOX 5 Atlanta, GPB, and the study’s journal home fill in the details: medical cannabis decriminalization was associated with about a 6.9 percent reduction in the likelihood of health-related work absences, while recreational cannabis laws showed no statistically significant effect.

The paper, “Cannabis laws and health-related workplace absenteeism in the United States,” was published June 10, 2026, in the Journal of Workplace Behavioral Health. Authors Eklou R. Amendah, Patryk Babiarz, and Kavitha Rabindran used monthly Current Population Survey data from January 1990 through March 2025—coverage FOX 5 described as spanning responses connected to more than 20 million people in Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics data. They applied a difference-in-differences approach that leveraged state-level timing of cannabis law changes and the onset of regulated sales.

According to the journal abstract, medical cannabis decriminalization reduced the probability of reporting health-related work absenteeism by about 6.9 percent when evaluated at the sample mean. Decriminalization showed a larger quantitative effect and greater statistical significance than the commencement of regulated medical sales. Recreational cannabis legalization did not show a significant effect on health-related workplace absenteeism in their models. Effects appeared most notable in occupations and industries where physical strain—and conditions sometimes associated with cannabis treatment, such as chronic pain—are more common.

This is population-level policy research, not a clinical trial assigning cannabis to individual workers. FOX 5’s reporting notes that the dataset relied on self-reported survey responses, which prevents direct verification that any one person’s absence dropped because they used cannabis for pain. Researchers did not measure the length of each sick-leave spell the way a human-resources audit might, and they did not evaluate how employer zero-tolerance drug policies interact with attendance.

Corresponding author Patryk Babiarz framed economic stakes in interviews: work absenteeism carries large productivity costs, and he argued medical marijuana access could save hundreds of millions of dollars annually if fewer people miss work for health reasons. That is an economic interpretation—not medical advice. California’s adult-use and medical markets mean many San Jose workers already buy from licensed retailers when rules allow. This UGA paper remains useful locally because it separates medical-law associations from recreational-law associations. A licensed dispensary receipt does not rewrite an employer’s drug-testing rules or fitness-for-duty standards. In San Jose’s limited storefront market, preparation beats impulse: verify the shop on SJPD’s registered cannabis businesses list, bring ID, read the package yourself after any recommendation, and keep consumption on private property with permission. Adult-use sales require customers 21 and older. Tax totals at checkout still apply. None of those retail realities replace clinician advice when health questions are personal.
