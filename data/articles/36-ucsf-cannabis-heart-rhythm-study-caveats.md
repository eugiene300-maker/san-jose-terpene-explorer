# UCSF Cannabis Heart-Rhythm Study—Caveats for Wellness Readers
**Dateline:** San Jose — September 2026
**Word count:** 719

**Image alt:** Abstract heartbeat graphic illustrating UCSF cannabis heart-rhythm research
**Image credit:** UC San Francisco news — https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2026/08/432341/unexpected-findings-add-new-insight-cannabis-and-heart-health

*A Bay Area trial found fewer premature heartbeats on inhalation days among habitual users. UCSF scientists said not to treat the result as medical advice—and shoppers should echo that hedge.*

KTVU highlighted in mid-August 2026 a UC San Francisco cannabis study with unexpected heart-rhythm findings—coverage that sent many Bay Area adults looking for the underlying research. UCSF’s news release, dated Aug. 12, 2026, describes a randomized crossover trial in which habitual cannabis users experienced about 9 percent fewer premature heartbeats on days they inhaled cannabis compared with days they abstained. The study was published Aug. 12 in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology.

For wellness-curious dispensary shoppers in San Jose and the South Bay, the responsible frame is curiosity plus caution. Researchers reported a statistically significant association in a specific group under short-term monitoring. They did not green-light cannabis as heart medicine, and UCSF’s release states plainly that the findings should not influence medical or behavioral decisions around cannabis use.

The MARY-JANE trial enrolled 108 adults who regularly smoked or vaped cannabis and had no history of clinically diagnosed cardiac arrhythmias. Over 14 days, participants were randomly assigned each day to inhale cannabis or abstain while wearing a continuously recording electrocardiographic monitor, plus activity, sleep, and continuous glucose monitors. Participants served as their own controls.

Researchers reported that cannabis inhalation was associated with a statistically significant reduction in cardiac ectopy—extra or early heartbeats—driven by fewer premature atrial contractions (PACs). Related reporting describes about a 10 percent reduction in PAC frequency, while premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) did not show a significant change. The journal abstract describes an overall 9 percent reduction in ectopic beats (rate ratio 0.91) and no significant differences for step counts, sleep duration, or glucose levels. Those null findings surprised investigators as well.

Gregory Marcus, MD, MAS, of UCSF’s Division of Cardiology, said the team was surprised because results contradicted the original hypothesis and stressed that randomized trials matter when data challenge expectations. He emphasized that the study examined acute effects in people who already use cannabis regularly and was not designed to evaluate long-term health consequences. “The overall health effects of inhaled cannabis may still be net negative,” he said in UCSF’s release, while noting that PAC biology might still teach researchers something useful.

This trial focused on inhaled cannabis among habitual users. It was not an edibles study. Adults who prefer gummies or beverages should not assume the same short-term electrocardiographic pattern applies. San Jose licensed retailers sell multiple form factors under California testing and labeling rules—helpful for potency transparency, not a substitute for clinical guidance. People with diagnosed arrhythmias were outside the enrolled population. Accurate counter language helps: say researchers reported fewer PACs on inhalation days in this trial, not “weed fixes your heart.” Say acute, short-term monitoring, not long-term proof.
