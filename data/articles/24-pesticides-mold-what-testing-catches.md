# Pesticides, Mold, and What Cannabis Testing Is Supposed to Catch
**Dateline:** San Jose — September 2026
**Word count:** 723

*Lab panels are a safety net—not a flavor guarantee—and illicit product skips the net entirely.*

When San Jose shoppers pay legal-market prices, part of the premium is invisible: laboratory screening meant to catch contaminants before product hits a registered counter. Understanding what testing is designed to catch—and what it cannot promise—helps explain why untested bargains are a poor trade.

California requires licensed cannabis goods to pass category-appropriate tests at licensed labs before retail sale. Contaminant categories described in state regulatory materials include pesticides, residual solvents for certain extracts, microbial impurities, mycotoxins, and heavy metals, alongside potency analysis for cannabinoid content. Exact analyte lists and action levels live in California Code of Regulations and DCC guidance; consumers do not need to memorize chemical tables, but they should know the direction of the policy: keep unsafe lots off shelves.

Mold and moisture problems show up in microbial and mycotoxin contexts. Flower that was poorly dried or stored can develop issues that legal testing is designed to flag. Illicit product can look fine and still carry mold risk—eyesight is not a lab. Pesticide residue is similarly invisible. Agricultural chemicals that may be inappropriate for inhaled products are a core reason regulated markets require screening.

What testing does not catch: every possible adulterant imaginable, future storage abuse after you take product home, or counterfeit packaging that never entered the legal chain. A legal label describes a batch that passed required panels at a point in time. Leaving a cart in a hot car can create new problems the lab never saw. Buying a lookalike cart from a social-media seller can skip testing entirely.

San Jose’s buy-legal messaging from SJPD ties registration to tested, labeled product and complaint pathways. That is consumer protection language, not marketing fluff. If a product smells harshly chemical, looks fuzzy, or makes you unexpectedly ill, stop using it, keep the package, contact the retailer, and consider DCC’s complaint process.

Home storage extends the safety chain: cool, dry, dark, child-resistant, and sealed. Testing cannot save product you store badly. Budtenders can discuss fresher harvest dates; they cannot reverse illicit supply chains.

The practical takeaway is sober rather than scary. Legal testing reduces specific, documented risks. It is one of the clearest differences between an SJPD-registered retailer and an informal bag. If price is the only scoreboard, untested product will often win. If lungs and accountability matter, the lab sticker is part of the product.
